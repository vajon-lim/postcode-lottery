FIT2179 Data Visualisation — Australia

The Postcode Lottery

In Australia, the healthcare you receive depends as much on your address as on your health. Explore the growing divide between city and remote Australians.

½ as many GP visits per person in very remote areas compared to major cities
58% of remote Australians report no nearby specialist — vs 6% in major cities
2.8× more preventable hospitalisations in very remote areas than in major cities

01

Where You Live Shapes Your Healthcare

Australia classifies its communities on a scale from MM1 (major cities) to MM7 (very remote outback). The map below shows how GP access drops sharply as you move away from urban centres. The further from a city, the harder it is to see a doctor.

Medical Practitioners Across Australia

Medical practitioners per 100,000 people by state and territory, 2023

The Northern Territory and South Australia have the highest concentrations of medical practitioners per capita. But state-level averages mask the real story: the divide is not between states, it's between cities and the country within each state.

Health Workforce by Remoteness

FTE practitioners per remoteness category, 2023

Every health profession is concentrated in cities. The log scale is necessary because the gap spans from over 90,000 FTE medical practitioners in major cities to just a handful in very remote areas. Some professions are virtually absent outside urban centres.

02

The GP Desert

General practitioners are the gateway to the healthcare system. But in remote and very remote communities, there are barely half the GPs per person compared to major cities — and that gap has been widening since 2019.

GP Numbers by Remoteness, 2020–2025

Total GPs per Modified Monash category

Major Cities have 22 times more GPs than Very Remote communities, and the gap has been growing. The two panels use independent scales; the break between them represents the full size of the divide. Regional towns saw modest growth; very remote communities almost none.

GPs per 100,000 People

GP clinical FTE per 100,000 population, by remoteness category, 2023

Per capita, many remote areas actually have comparable or more GPs than cities, as small populations and government incentive programs push the rate up. The real gap is in very small rural towns (MM5), which have the fewest GPs per person, caught between urban supply and remote-area programs.

GP Workforce Composition

Vocationally registered vs trainees, 2025

Over a third of GPs in regional towns are trainees on mandatory rural training rotations, not permanent practitioners. Major Cities run on 92% fully qualified GPs.

03

Getting Care When You Need It

The shortage of providers has a direct human cost. Australians in outer regional and remote areas are significantly more likely to delay or miss healthcare they know they need — across GP visits, dental care, mental health, and emergency services.

Difficulty Accessing GP Care Over Time

% of people who needed GP care but faced access barriers, 2013–2025 (ABS Patient Experience Survey)

About 1 in 4 Australians who needed a GP faced some access barrier every year. Since 2021, cost has driven a sharp rise: as bulk billing declined, cost-related barriers nearly quadrupled. More people are now skipping the doctor because they can't afford the gap fee.

Access Difficulty Across Services

% who needed care but could not access it, by service type (ABS Patient Experience Survey)

After-hours GP care is by far the hardest service to access: in 2022-23, 38% of people who needed it could not get it. Dental care has persistently high unmet need at around 14-17%. Regular GP appointments remain the easiest to access, with under 2% missing out, but the gap widens sharply for after-hours and specialist care.

Who Struggles Most to Access Care

% who needed services, by age group: GP, Dental and Mental Health, 2024-25

Mental health service use is highest among young adults (15-34) and declines sharply with age, reflecting both need and access patterns. GP use rises steadily with age, with nearly all Australians aged 75+ seeing a doctor each year. Dental visits remain relatively consistent across adult age groups.

04

Beyond GPs — The Specialist Gap

The shortage extends well beyond general practice. Psychologists, dentists, physiotherapists and specialists are concentrated in major cities. In remote communities, many of these professions are nearly absent altogether.

Health Workforce FTE by Profession and Remoteness

FTE per 100,000 population by remoteness, 2023; each panel uses its own scale so smaller professions are as readable as Medical Practitioners

Each panel has its own scale, so small professions like Podiatrists are as readable as the dominant Medical workforce. Medical Practitioners peak at 516 per 100k in Major Cities and remain present everywhere. ATSI Health Practitioners are the opposite: nearly absent in cities (0.9 per 100k) but climbing to 68 per 100k in Very Remote areas. Grey 0 labels confirm that Psychologists, Paramedics, and Podiatrists have no reported presence beyond Major Cities.

The Urban-Regional Workforce Gap, by Profession

FTE per 100,000 population; blue dot = Major Cities, orange dot = Outer Regional (orange dot on the axis means zero practitioners)

Medical practitioners remain the most abundant workforce in Outer Regional areas at 316 per 100,000, though that is 39% below Major Cities. Dental practitioners and chiropractors also retain a partial presence. Psychologists, paramedicine practitioners, and podiatrists have zero practitioners in Outer Regional areas. The one exception is ATSI Health Practitioners: just 0.9 per 100,000 in Major Cities but 10.8 in Outer Regional, a 12-fold increase reflecting this workforce's specific deployment to underserved communities.

05

Victoria Up Close

Even within a single state, the postcode lottery plays out. Victoria's inner metropolitan areas enjoy far better access to primary care than its regional communities. The map below uses PHIDU data to show emergency department presentation rates — a signal of where people lack adequate local GP and specialist services, forcing them into hospital emergency rooms.

Emergency Department Presentations Across Victoria

Age-standardised ED visits per 100,000 people by SA3 area; lighter shading = better primary care access

Victoria's outer west and north (areas with limited local GPs) show the highest emergency department use. When people can't access primary care, the hospital ED becomes the fallback. High ED rates are often a symptom of inadequate GP access, not just higher illness rates.

Population Size vs Health Outcomes

Each point = one Victorian Population Health Area; trend lines show the direction for each group

The linear trend line for Regional Victoria sits above Metro Melbourne's across the full population range, confirming that regional communities face higher ED presentation rates at every scale. Metro Melbourne's line trends downward as areas grow larger, reflecting better access to alternatives. The dots are faded to 30% opacity to keep the trend lines as the primary story.

06

The Human Scale

Behind every percentage point is a real person who couldn't see a doctor when they needed one. Each square below represents one Australian in 100.

The Regional Penalty in Healthcare Access

Based on ABS Patient Experiences Survey 2024–25

Location determines access. In major cities, 24 out of 100 people went without GP care when they needed it. In regional and remote areas, that number climbs to 30, meaning remote residents face a 25% higher risk of missing out on vital medical attention.
