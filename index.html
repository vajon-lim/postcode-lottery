FIT2179 Data Visualisation II

The Postcode Lottery

In Australia, the healthcare you receive depends as much on your address as on your health. This is a data-driven look at the widening geographical divide and the structural barriers reshaping who gets care, and who gets left behind.

50% Fewer GP visits per capita in very remote areas versus major cities.
58% Remote Australians report having no nearby specialist (compared to just 6% in major cities).
2.8× Higher rate of preventable hospitalisations in very remote areas compared to major cities.

01

Where You Live Shapes Your Healthcare

When we look at healthcare access across Australia, geography is destiny. But as the map below shows, looking simply at state and territory averages doesn't tell the whole story.

State Averages Hide the Local Reality

Medical practitioners per 100,000 people by state and territory, 2023

The Northern Territory and Tasmania boast the highest concentrations of medical practitioners per capita. However, state-level averages mask the real story. The true divide isn't between states, it's between cities and the country within them. To see this, we must look at Australia's remoteness scale, ranging from major cities (MM1) to the very remote outback (MM7).

The Geographic Divide in Health Professions

Full-Time Equivalent (FTE) practitioners, 2023 (Log Scale)

Health professionals are overwhelmingly concentrated in major urban centres. A log scale is required to visualise this massive disparity side-by-side: while cities host tens of thousands of medical practitioners, remote areas rely on just a handful. Specialised roles, such as psychologists and podiatrists, become practically absent as remoteness increases.

02

The GP Desert

General practitioners are the gateway to the healthcare system. While remote areas receive targeted government support, residents in smaller rural towns are caught in a growing "GP desert," facing severe per-capita shortages compared to their metropolitan counterparts.

GP Numbers by Remoteness, 2020–2025

Total GPs per Modified Monash category

Major Cities have 22 times more GPs than Very Remote communities, and the gap has been growing. The two panels use independent scales; the break between them represents the full size of the divide. Regional towns saw modest growth; very remote communities almost none.

GPs per 100,000 People

GP clinical FTE per 100,000 population, by remoteness category, 2023

Per capita, many remote areas actually have comparable or more GPs than cities, as small populations and government incentive programs push the rate up. The real gap is in very small rural towns (MM5), which have the fewest GPs per person, caught between urban supply and remote-area programs.

GP Workforce Composition

Vocationally registered vs trainees, 2025

Over a third of GPs in regional towns are trainees on mandatory rural training rotations, not permanent practitioners. Major Cities run on 92% fully qualified GPs.

03

Getting Care When You Need It

The shortage of providers has a direct human cost. Even beyond geography, everyday Australians are increasingly forced to delay or miss the healthcare they need. Driven by rising costs and severe shortages in specific sectors like after-hours care, access barriers are shifting dramatically across different services and age groups.

Difficulty Accessing GP Care Over Time

% of people who needed GP care but faced access barriers, 2013–2025 (ABS Patient Experience Survey)

Historically, roughly 1 in 4 Australians who needed a GP faced some form of access barrier. However, the nature of these barriers is shifting. Since reaching a pandemic low, cost-related barriers have surged dramatically, nearly quadrupling as financial pressures increasingly force patients to skip needed medical care.

Access Difficulty Across Services

% who needed care but could not access it, by service type (ABS Patient Experience Survey)

After-hours GP care is by far the hardest service to access: in 2022-23, 38% of people who needed it could not get it. Dental care has persistently high unmet need at around 14-17%. Regular GP appointments remain the easiest to access, with under 2% missing out, but the gap widens sharply for after-hours and specialist care.

Healthcare Demand by Age

% who needed services, by age group: GP, Dental and Mental Health, 2024-25

Healthcare reliance shifts drastically across a lifetime. Mental health service usage peaks sharply among young adults (15-34) before declining with age. Conversely, reliance on general practitioners rises steadily as Australians get older, with nearly all residents aged 75+ requiring a doctor each year. Meanwhile, the need for dental care remains relatively constant across all adult demographics.

04

Beyond GPs: The Specialist Gap

The shortage extends well beyond general practice. Specialised fields like psychology, dentistry, and allied health are heavily concentrated in major cities. In remote communities, several of these professions report zero workforce presence.

Health Workforce Full-Time Equivalents (FTE) by Profession and Remoteness

FTE per 100,000 population by remoteness, 2023; each panel uses its own scale so smaller professions are as readable as Medical Practitioners

Because workforce sizes vary vastly, each panel uses an independent scale. Medical Practitioners maintain a baseline presence everywhere, peaking at 516 per 100k in Major Cities. Conversely, First Nations Health Practitioners follow an inverse pattern: nearly absent in cities (0.9 per 100k) but surging to 68 per 100k in Very Remote areas. For Psychologists, Paramedicine Practitioners, and Podiatrists, the data shows no reported presence beyond Major Cities.

The Urban-Regional Workforce Gap, by Profession

FTE per 100,000 population; blue dot = Major Cities, orange dot = Outer Regional (orange dot on the axis means zero practitioners)

Even comparing Major Cities directly to Outer Regional areas reveals a stark disparity. The medical practitioner workforce drops by nearly 40% per capita outside major centres, while dental and chiropractic care also decline. As established, psychologists, paramedicine practitioners, and podiatrists show zero reported presence. The clear outlier is the First Nations Health Practitioner workforce, which sees a 12-fold increase, from just 0.9 per 100,000 in Major Cities to 10.8 in Outer Regional areas, reflecting targeted deployment to underserved communities.

05

Victoria Up Close: The Hospital Spillover

Even within a single state, the postcode lottery plays out. Victoria's inner metropolitan areas enjoy far better access to primary care than its regional communities. The map below illustrates emergency department presentation rates, a strong indicator of where a lack of adequate local GP and specialist services is forcing residents into hospital emergency rooms.

Emergency Department Presentations Across Victoria

Age-standardised ED visits per 100,000 people by SA3 area (lighter shading = fewer ED visits)

Victoria's outer west and north (areas with limited local GPs) show the highest emergency department use. When people can't access primary care, the hospital ED becomes the fallback. High ED rates are often a symptom of inadequate GP access, not just higher illness rates.

The Regional Burden on Emergency Departments

Each dot represents a local community (Population Health Area). The trend lines show the average trajectory for each region.

Across all population sizes, the trend line for Regional Victoria sits distinctly above Metro Melbourne's, confirming that regional communities carry a heavier burden of ED presentations. This gap is most severe in smaller population centres, with the disparity gradually narrowing as local area populations grow.

06

The Human Scale

Behind every statistic is a real person who struggled to see a doctor when they needed one. In the charts below, each square represents one Australian in 100.

The Regional Penalty in Healthcare Access

Proportion of people who needed GP care but delayed or missed it, 2024–25 (ABS)

Location dictates access. In major cities, 26 out of every 100 people who needed a GP delayed or missed their care. In regional and remote areas, that number climbs to 30. This means residents living outside major urban centres face a 15% higher relative risk of missing out on, or being forced to delay, vital medical attention.
